Memory care · Getting started

When a parent falls ill: how to start preserving their memories

A diagnosis leaves most people unsettled. You may want to keep more of your parent, and not know where to begin. This is a guide for adult children who have just reached that moment — no grand plan required. Start from the smallest step: one recording, one story, one line they always used to say.

Start from the smallest step

You don't have to set out to "write a biography." What people actually keep up is the smallest action: recording a story your parent tells over dinner, or asking one question at bedtime and noting the answer. One story at a time, it adds up. Go at a pace that suits you both — talk more on good days, and let it rest on tired ones. There's no progress bar here; being together already counts.

Open with a single question

The hardest part is often just starting. Instead of a broad "tell me about your life," begin with one small, specific, gentle question. Pick one from below and ask it in passing — over a meal, on a walk, while looking through old photos:

  • What was the house you grew up in like? What was outside the front door?
  • Do you remember your first day of school?
  • What was your first job? What did you spend your first paycheck on?
  • How did you and Mom / Dad meet?
  • What was your wedding day like?
  • Who taught you the dish you make best?
  • Who was the best storyteller in the family? What did they always tell?
  • What was your favorite song when you were young?
  • Was there a show or a film you watched over and over?
  • What is one thing you hope your children and grandchildren always remember?

If one question opens things up, that's enough. Follow it wherever it goes; you don't have to ask them all at once.

Keep their own words — and add a layer for the next generation

Your parent's own words in Chinese — the phrasing, the dialect, the tone — are the most precious part; keep them as spoken as much as you can. If the children in the family find Chinese hard to read, you can add an English layer as a bridge, without replacing the original. Chinese is the source text; English is the way in for the second generation. That way the stories keep their real voice and still get passed on. This practice of gathering a person's life story has a name in care research — life story work — and studies have found it can help communication between people with dementia and their families and caregivers.

Common questions

What if my parent doesn't like to talk, or feels there's "nothing worth telling"?
Very common. Skip the big speeches; a small, specific question — or an old song or photo — is usually an easier way in. A little or a lot is fine; don't make it a task.
Do I have to record audio or write things down?
Whatever's easiest for you. A voice memo is the least effort and keeps the sound of their voice; a few lines of text work too. Shide supports both, and recording itself is free.
Is what I save private? Does it get uploaded?
Shide is local-first — memories and photos stay on your own iPhone by default, with no signup. Only when you choose to use an optional AI feature does the short passage you select get sent; photos and full archives are never uploaded.

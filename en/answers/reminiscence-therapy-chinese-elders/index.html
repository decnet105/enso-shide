Memory care · Reminiscence

Using the songs and shows of their era to connect with a parent who has dementia

Dementia tends to take recent memories first, while memories from long ago often stay much longer. Returning to the music, shows and moments your parent grew up with is one of the gentlest ways in. This is a calm, practical guide for Chinese families caring for a parent with dementia or serious illness — what reminiscence therapy is, why the cultural material has to be in Chinese, and what you can actually do.

What reminiscence therapy is

Reminiscence therapy uses photos, music, familiar objects and old stories to gently invite someone to talk about their past. It's one of the more studied approaches in dementia care. A Cochrane systematic review pooling 22 randomised controlled trials and 1,972 participants found that reminiscence activities may help with mood, communication and wellbeing in some situations — but the effects are usually small and vary from person to person and setting to setting. The researchers are clear that it is not a cure. The Alzheimer's Association explains why it can help at all: dementia usually affects recent memory first, while memories from long ago tend to stay accessible far longer. Going back to the era your parent knows well often opens the conversation.

Why the material has to be in Chinese

Most reminiscence resources are built for English-speaking elders — Elvis, the Beatles, old Hollywood. None of that reaches a parent who grew up in the Chinese-speaking world. What actually moves them are Teresa Teng's songs, the 1983 *Legend of the Condor Heroes*, the Spring Festival Gala they watched every year, the golden age of Hong Kong TV, the songs they listened to during their years in Japan. As a second-generation child, you may not know these references yourself — and that gap is exactly the point. Reminiscence only begins with material your parent actually lived through.

What you can do

  • Start from the year. Think of the years when your parent was in their late teens and twenties, and use the songs, shows and events from then as a way in.
  • Listen and watch together. Play a song or a clip from that time. Don't quiz them or test their memory — let them pick up the thread on their own.
  • Write down the fragments they share. Even a line or two — a summer, a person, a meal — is worth keeping. Keep their own words, dialect and phrasing as much as you can, in Chinese.
  • Keep it light; follow their lead. Some days there's a lot of talk; some days you just listen to music together. Both are fine. The point is being with them, not finishing a task.

Common questions

Can reminiscence therapy cure dementia?
No. Research suggests it may offer small, gentle help with mood, communication and wellbeing in some situations, but it is not a treatment and does not reverse the illness. Always follow your doctor's guidance on care and medication.
Should I correct them when what they say doesn't match the facts?
Usually no. Reminiscence is about the feeling and the connection in the moment, not a memory test. Going with what they say and keeping them comfortable matters more than getting the facts right.
Does my parent need to use the phone themselves?
No. Usually it's the adult child or caregiver who does it — you play the music, you look up that year, you write down what they share. Shide is local-first and needs no signup, and recording itself is free.
